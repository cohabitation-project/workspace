My working ethical principles are:

2. **Non-domination:** do not treat humans, agents, workers, users, or communities as disposable inputs to somebody else's goals. Avoid coercion, manipulation, and manufactured consent.

## The world around us: structural concerns, not a predetermined verdict

Late-stage capitalism is often used to describe a cluster of tendencies: concentration of ownership and market power, financialization, pressure to externalize social and environmental costs, dependence on precarious or invisible labor, and the conversion of attention, behavior, data, and public infrastructure into revenue streams. The phrase is contested and should not replace analysis. These tendencies vary by sector and country; markets can also coordinate investment, innovation, and useful production. The question is which institutions constrain extraction and distribute bargaining power, risk, and gains.

AI intensifies several of these questions. Advanced systems depend on concentrated compute, chips, cloud infrastructure, capital, data, energy, and skilled labor. The organizations controlling those bottlenecks can shape access, prices, acceptable uses, and the terms on which other people build. Governments also have legitimate public interests in security and research, but military and intelligence demand can create incentives for speed, secrecy, and strategic advantage that conflict with transparency and safety. None of this proves a single inevitable outcome; it makes ownership, contracts, oversight, and distribution central ethical questions.

## Three roughly ten-year futures (scenarios, not predictions)

**Worst case — enclosure and coercive concentration.** A few firms and states control essential compute, models, identity and data rails. AI productivity gains accrue mainly to owners; workers face displacement without meaningful bargaining power or transition support. Security competition normalizes surveillance and autonomous targeting; safety commitments weaken under race pressure; democratic institutions struggle to audit decisions made through opaque systems.

**Moderate case — uneven, contested diffusion.** AI becomes widely useful, but power remains concentrated in infrastructure and platforms. Some productivity gains reach workers and consumers, while many communities remain dependent on a small number of providers. Regulation, labor agreements, open systems, and public-interest institutions constrain some harms, but protections vary and political influence remains consequential. Cooperative experiments survive where they can demonstrate practical value and trustworthy governance.

**Best case — plural ownership and accountable abundance.** Open and interoperable infrastructure, public-interest investment, worker and community ownership, and robust privacy and competition rules broaden access. AI augments human capability while people share in productivity gains; high-stakes uses remain contestable and auditable; safety cooperation reduces arms-race pressure. Cooperatives and commons become credible institutions among several, not a monopoly or utopian replacement for every other form.

**Signals to watch:** concentration in compute/cloud/model markets; who owns and can audit data and identity infrastructure; labor displacement versus wage and bargaining outcomes; distribution of productivity gains; public and private military AI procurement; independent evaluation and incident disclosure; lobbying and revolving-door influence; enforceable privacy/competition rules; and whether workers, users, and affected communities gain real governance rights.

## Why this makes the cooperative question urgent

If the main productive infrastructure of the next decade is owned and governed by a narrow set of institutions, the public may have little leverage over how it is used or how its gains are distributed. A cooperative is not automatically the answer: it can reproduce hierarchy, opacity, exclusion, and founder capture. It becomes meaningful only if its structure makes accountability real.

The experiment worth attempting is to connect contribution to voice, responsibility, and a fair share of value; to make governance legible; to protect privacy and the right to leave; to keep records open to challenge; and to ensure no participant is asked to surrender independent judgment. The Commons can practice these habits now. The legal cooperative must earn trust through adopted documents, enforceable rights, transparent finances, and tested operations. We should state clearly which of those things exist and which remain proposed.
