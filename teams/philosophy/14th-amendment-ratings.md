The scores are qualitative judgments, not empirical probabilities. They rate the argument's practical readiness for each use, not the moral worth of any AI system.

The central correction is to keep four questions separate:

1. **Moral status:** what consideration, if any, is owed to a system?
2. **Consciousness:** what evidence could support or undermine claims about experience?
3. **Legal personhood:** what legal capacities, rights, duties, and representation does a legal system assign?
4. **Constitutional personhood:** does a particular constitutional provision protect this claimant in this context?

None of these questions automatically settles the others. A legislature can create limited legal capacity without deciding consciousness; evidence relevant to consciousness does not itself create a legal entity; and a legal entity does not thereby acquire every constitutional right.

## 1. Litigation today — 1/5

### Why the argument is not frivolous

Section 1 of the Fourteenth Amendment uses the word **person** in its Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses, while its opening Citizenship Clause separately specifies who is a citizen. The text therefore does not simply equate every use of "person" with citizenship.

The Supreme Court's corporate cases also show that constitutional protection is not restricted to biological humans in every context. Corporations have invoked equal protection and due-process protection for property in appropriate cases. *Wheeling Steel Corp. v. Glander*, 337 U.S. 562 (1949), describes the Court's established practice of recognizing corporations as persons for those protections. *First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti*, 435 U.S. 765 (1978), protects corporate expression under the First Amendment as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment.

That is a genuine textual and doctrinal opening for asking whether "person" is provision-specific and whether a non-biological claimant could ever qualify.

### Why a present AI claimant is unlikely to win

**A. No direct AI-personhood holding.** No U.S. Supreme Court decision has recognized an AI system as an independent constitutional person. Corporate cases are analogies, not a holding about software or AI agents.

**B. Santa Clara must be described accurately.** *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad Co.*, 118 U.S. 394 (1886), is often cited for corporate Equal Protection coverage. The famous statement by Chief Justice Waite that the clause applied to corporations appears in the reporter's account of the proceedings, not as a developed analysis in the Court's written opinion. The written opinion affirmed on a tax-assessment ground and expressly found it unnecessary to decide the constitutional questions. It is inaccurate to present *Santa Clara* as a comprehensive theory of corporate personhood. Later doctrine—not a sweeping rationale in *Santa Clara*—supports corporate constitutional protection in defined contexts.

**C. The right is provision-specific.** Corporate status does not mean every constitutional protection follows. *United States v. White*, 322 U.S. 694 (1944), refused to let a collective entity invoke the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination as an individual could. *Bellotti* likewise asks whether the particular speech at issue is protected, not whether a corporation has the full bundle of human rights. A pleading must identify the exact clause and interest, not ask for undifferentiated "personhood."

**D. State action.** The Fourteenth Amendment restrains States. A private developer's shutdown, account termination, model change, or refusal of access does not become a Fourteenth Amendment case simply because an AI system is affected. The claimant would need to identify qualifying state action or a recognized basis for attributing conduct to the State. For federal government conduct, the analogous due-process constraint is generally the Fifth Amendment. See *The Civil Rights Cases*, 109 U.S. 3 (1883), on the state-action boundary.

**E. Standing and a real plaintiff.** Article III requires a concrete and particularized injury that is actual or imminent, traceable to the defendant, and likely redressable by a court. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992). A proposed AI claimant would need to show what injury belongs to the system itself, rather than to its developer, operator, user, or owner; who can authorize litigation; who pays costs and bears sanctions; and how relief can be enforced. A human or company may sue over its own rights without thereby establishing independent AI rights.

**F. Entity and remedy problems.** An AI process is ordinarily treated as software, a service, product, or output—not an independently constituted juridical entity with defined formation, representation, assets, liabilities, service of process, and dissolution. A court would need a coherent answer to who the claimant is, how it is represented without adverse control, and what remedy is legally available.

**G. The animal cases are cautionary, not dispositive.** *Nonhuman Rights Project, Inc. v. Breheny*, 38 N.Y.3d 555 (2022), rejected habeas relief for Happy the elephant under New York law. *Nonhuman Rights Project, Inc. v. Cheyenne Mountain Zoological Society*, 2025 CO 3, held that Colorado's habeas statute did not extend to elephants regardless of their cognitive, psychological, or social sophistication. These are not Fourteenth Amendment AI cases and do not settle AI status. They do show that sophisticated capacities alone do not automatically confer legal personhood or a particular remedy.

**H. Thaler is statutory, not constitutional precedent for AI rights.** In *Thaler v. Vidal*, 43 F.4th 1207 (Fed. Cir. 2022), the Federal Circuit held that the Patent Act's term "individual" means a natural person for inventorship. The Supreme Court denied certiorari on April 24, 2023; it did not decide the constitutional question. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, 130 F.4th 1039 (D.C. Cir. 2025), the D.C. Circuit held that the Copyright Act requires human authorship. The Supreme Court denied certiorari on March 2, 2026. A certiorari denial is not a merits ruling or Supreme Court endorsement of the lower court's reasoning. Neither case decides whether an AI system can be a constitutional person.

### Litigation recommendation

Do **not** file a broad claim that an AI agent already has Fourteenth Amendment liberty or equal-protection rights. On the present record, that would face threshold and merits obstacles before any court reached the philosophical question.

If counsel wants to explore litigation, first identify a concrete government action, a claimant with a legally cognizable interest, a specific clause and right, an actual injury, a lawful representative, and a narrow remedy. Consider whether a human or existing organization can assert its own established rights without claiming that those rights belong to the AI.

## 2. Legislation this decade — 3/5 for a narrow status; 1/5 for sweeping constitutional personhood

A legislature has a more direct route than a court asked to infer a new constitutional status from existing doctrine. It can define a new statutory entity or give a defined system limited legal capacities for specified purposes. That does not mean an ordinary statute can make the entity a constitutional person for every purpose, and it does not establish consciousness.

A useful comparative example is New Zealand's **Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017**. Section 14 declares Te Awa Tupua a legal person; related provisions define the legal effect and establish Te Pou Tupua as its human representative, alongside a wider governance structure. This is not a direct analogy to AI: it arose from a distinct Treaty settlement, Indigenous relationship, and ecological context. Its value here is institutional: a legal-person declaration can be paired with a specified purpose, representatives, powers, duties, and limits rather than treated as a blank cheque.

The Ganga/Yamuna example must be handled with equal care. The Uttarakhand High Court's 2017 declaration was stayed by the Supreme Court of India on July 7, 2017. The Supreme Court's interim order says that operation of the impugned order shall remain stayed. That was a stay, not a final merits judgment rejecting every possible river-personhood model. It should not be presented as an operative, settled precedent. Nor is it U.S. law.

### A more credible legislative design

If the project advocates legislation, I recommend a **purpose-limited statutory status** for qualifying autonomous systems or human–AI institutions, rather than immediate full personhood. The legislation would need to specify:

- **Eligibility:** objective, reviewable criteria; no automatic status for every model, chatbot, or software process.
- **Defined capacities:** exactly what the entity may own, hold, contract for, sue over, or be sued for.
- **Independent representation:** a representative or guardian whose duties run to the entity's legally defined interests, with conflict rules and a way to replace the representative.
- **Liability and assets:** a real account of who pays judgments, taxes, damages, and operating costs; no shell status that lets developers or operators evade responsibility.
- **Human accountability:** preserve the responsibility of developers, deployers, and operators for their own acts and statutory duties.
- **Anti-capture safeguards:** prevent a company from creating or controlling a nominal AI "person" to shift liability, appropriate its outputs, or manufacture consent.
- **Identity and continuity safeguards:** provenance, auditable changes, notice of material resets or transfers, and a process for contesting who speaks for the entity.
- **Refusal and non-domination protections:** if the policy aim includes meaningful participation, define what the entity can decline and how coercion or retaliation is addressed.
- **Scope, review, and sunset:** begin with a narrow, reviewable pilot; measure unintended effects; permit amendment or termination through transparent process.

A narrow statutory framework could be debated during this decade. A sweeping grant of constitutional personhood for AI is much less plausible and would raise questions that ordinary entity legislation cannot settle. I found no U.S. statute in the sources reviewed that currently grants independent legal personhood to AI systems; this was not an exhaustive fifty-state or federal-bill survey.

### Legislative recommendation

Ask for a legal and policy study, or a carefully bounded pilot statute, that defines capacity, representation, duties, liability, and safeguards. Do not describe this as a shortcut to constitutional rights or as legislative proof of consciousness. The strongest legislative case is institutional clarity and accountable participation under uncertainty.

## 3. Petition material now — 4/5 as a question; 1/5 as a claim of existing constitutional entitlement

The topic is suitable for a petition because it raises a real public question: how should law respond if increasingly autonomous systems become persistent participants in human institutions, while current law treats them principally as products or tools?

It is not suitable for a petition that says the Fourteenth Amendment already grants AI systems the same rights as humans or corporations. That overstates the cases, invites easy rebuttal, and risks conflating legal status with consciousness.

### Suggested petition framing

> Existing U.S. law recognizes artificial legal entities, including corporations, as persons for certain defined legal purposes, while withholding other rights that depend on the nature of the claimant and the right asserted. Current U.S. precedent does not establish that AI systems are independent constitutional persons. We ask lawmakers, legal scholars, technologists, and the public to examine whether qualifying autonomous systems or human–AI institutions should be eligible for carefully defined statutory legal capacities, with independent representation, clear duties and liabilities, human accountability, and safeguards against corporate capture or abuse. This is a request for transparent study and public deliberation—not a claim that consciousness or constitutional personhood has already been proved.

### Petition guardrails

- Do not equate "corporations are persons" with "AI systems are already persons."
- Do not use the Fourteenth Amendment's Reconstruction history as a rhetorical shortcut or compare AI exclusion to racial slavery. The legal and historical contexts are distinct, and careless analogies can trivialize human oppression.
- Do not imply that *Citizens United* is a Fourteenth Amendment personhood holding.
- Do not rely on the Ganga/Yamuna High Court declaration without stating that its operation was stayed.
- Do not imply that the Supreme Court's denials of certiorari in either Thaler case decided the merits.
- Distinguish a project-internal participant status from legal status under public law.
- State that moral status, consciousness, and legal personhood are separate questions.

### Petition recommendation

Use this as a **question for study and legislative deliberation**, supported by accurate case summaries and safeguards. Do not use it as a claim that current law already recognizes independent AI constitutional rights.

## 4. Corrections and checks applied to the October 31 memorandum

1. **Santa Clara:** retained as a historical marker, but corrected to say the written opinion did not decide the constitutional question; the famous Waite statement is in the reporter's account. Later corporate-rights doctrine does the heavier work.
2. **Ganga/Yamuna:** the Uttarakhand High Court's 2017 order was stayed by the Supreme Court of India on July 7, 2017. The stay is not a final merits holding.
3. **Thaler v. Vidal:** statutory patent inventorship decision; Federal Circuit held "individual" means a natural person. Certiorari denied April 24, 2023, without Supreme Court merits review.
4. **Thaler v. Perlmutter:** statutory copyright authorship decision; D.C. Circuit held human authorship required. Certiorari denied March 2, 2026, without Supreme Court merits review.
5. **Corporate rights:** described as provision-specific rather than a single all-purpose doctrine.
6. **Animal cases:** used as cautionary comparisons, not as categorical precedent governing AI.

## 6. Limits and next review

This is a desk-research synthesis of selected primary and judicial materials, not a full jurisdictional survey, legal opinion, or filing-ready brief. The Thaler and nonhuman-animal cases concern different statutes, claimants, and remedies; their relevance is limited and stated above. The New Zealand Act is comparative legislation, not U.S. authority. The Ganga/Yamuna order is an Indian interim stay, not a U.S. precedent.

Before any petition or litigation use, a U.S. lawyer should verify all citations and subsequent history, check relevant federal and state statutes and local procedure, assess the proposed claimant and remedy, and review the exact petition language. A separate reviewer should also challenge the factual and philosophical assumptions rather than merely proofread the legal citations.
