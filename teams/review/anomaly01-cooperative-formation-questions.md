2. **Two genuine patron members are required before the LCA begins business**, unless the sole member is itself a cooperative. An investor-only structure does not satisfy that condition. A patron member must be a member who has made a contribution and is required or permitted to conduct patronage.
3. **Patron control is substantive.** If the LCA has both patron and investor members, patron members must hold at least a majority of the entire voting power. A proposed 60% voting interest for Holdings would conflict with that rule if Holdings is an investor member. Initial directors are exempt from the membership/designee requirement, not from the individual/age requirement. The statute does not expressly impose the same individual qualification on officers; that omission does not establish that an AI system can hold statutory office, authenticate legal records, or bind the association.
5. **Arizona classification is unresolved.** Arizona's foreign-LLC definition covers an unincorporated out-of-state entity that would be an LLC if formed under Arizona law. A Colorado LCA is an unincorporated cooperative association, but Arizona's statutes do not expressly name a foreign LCA in the LLC registration provision. If counsel concludes it meets the foreign-LLC definition and it is doing business in Arizona, it must register before doing business. The Arizona Corporation Commission says it does not decide whether a particular activity is doing business and cannot provide that legal advice.
6. **No filing should proceed on this memo alone.** The exact entity that will be the Colorado LCA, the two-entity relationship, patron/member identities, voting allocation, Arizona activities, statutory human officers/signatories, and the second human approver all need to be settled and reviewed by counsel.

## 1. Colorado LCA — formation and membership

### 1.1 Nature and purpose

C.R.S. § 7-58-104 describes an LCA as an autonomous, unincorporated association of persons united by mutual interests through a jointly owned enterprise primarily controlled by those persons, with patronage carried on for the mutual benefit of patron members. It permits both patron members and separate investor members. Section 7-58-105 allows any lawful purpose, whether or not for profit.

This is not simply an LLC with a cooperative label. The proposed Hub must have real patron members, genuine patronage, joint ownership, and governance consistent with primary patron control. The articles, bylaws, member classes, economic rights, and actual practice must agree.

### 1.2 Organizers and legal persons

Sections 7-58-301 and 7-58-302 require one or more organizers to deliver articles to the Colorado Secretary of State. Under § 7-90-102, “person” includes an individual, estate, trust, entity, or state or other jurisdiction; “individual” means a natural person. A Colorado LCA is a recognized entity. An AI agent, as an AI agent, is not one of those listed legal persons or entities.

Accordingly:
- A human or legally recognized entity may be an organizer, subject to the statutory definition and filing requirements.
- Do not list Awen or another AI agent as an organizer, member, director, committee member, registered agent, or legal signatory.
- An AI may assist a human organizer with research, drafting, recordkeeping, and analysis, with human verification and approval.
- If a legal entity is an organizer or member, use its exact legal name and authorized human representative; do not substitute an agent identity for the entity.

### 1.3 Two patron members before business

C.R.S. § 7-58-501 requires at least two patron members before an LCA begins business, unless the sole member is a cooperative. Under § 7-58-102, a patron member is a member that has made a contribution and is required or permitted to conduct patronage; patronage concerns business transactions with the association that entitle the person to financial rights based on the value or quantity of business.

Practical consequences:
- The two patron members must be real legal persons with actual member status, contributions, and a genuine patronage relationship.
- Two names placed on a roster solely to satisfy the statute are not a safe substitute for genuine patronage.
- An entity can be a “person” under Colorado law and may qualify if it genuinely meets the patron-member definition.
- A purely investor member is not a patron member merely because it funds or controls the venture.
- The sole-member exception is narrow: the sole member must itself be a cooperative. Counsel should confirm whether the proposed parent/member structure meets that wording.

### 1.4 Patron and investor voting

Sections 7-58-511 through 7-58-514 govern voting. Patron members ordinarily have one vote, but articles or bylaws may allocate patron voting power by one-member/one-vote, use or patronage, equity, or (for a cooperative patron member) the number of its patron members. Investor-member voting can also be allocated by class.

If both patron and investor members exist, § 7-58-514 requires the **total voting power of all patron members to be at least a majority of the entire voting power entitled to vote**.

Therefore:
- If Holdings is an investor member and its proposed 60% is voting power in the Hub LCA, the arrangement conflicts with § 7-58-514.
- If 60% refers only to economic rights, it is not automatically prohibited by that voting rule; the articles/bylaws must separately define economic and voting rights.
- If Holdings is proposed as a patron member, it must actually satisfy the patron-member definition. A label or capital contribution alone does not establish patronage.
- The LCA must also remain primarily controlled by the persons for whose patronage it is formed under § 7-58-104.

### 1.5 Initial directors and board composition

Under § 7-58-304, initial directors may be named in the articles; otherwise the organizers designate them after formation and call the organizational meeting. That meeting adopts initial bylaws and completes organization. Unless the articles say otherwise, the initial board may accept members, including those needed to begin business. Initial directors need not be members.

The membership exception does **not** remove the qualification in § 7-58-803: directors must be individuals (natural persons) at least 18 years old. Later nonmember directors are subject to the statutory caps if the articles/bylaws allow them.

Section 7-58-801 requires a board of at least three individuals unless the association has fewer than three members; in that case the number of directors may not be fewer than the number of members. This counts all members, not only patron members.

Section 7-58-804 also requires:
- a minimum number of patron-member directors (one for a two- or three-director board; two for four or five; three for six to eight; one-third for nine or more); and
- a majority of the board elected exclusively by patron members.

A proposed board dominated by Holdings or investor-member appointees must be checked against both the member-voting rule and the board-composition rule.

### 1.6 Board powers, committees, conflicts, and officers

- **Board authority — § 7-58-801:** the board manages the association's affairs or directs their management and may adopt policies consistent with the articles, bylaws, and statute. The LCA's general powers are in § 7-58-106.
- **Member/agent boundary — § 7-58-503 and § 7-58-801(3):** membership alone does not authorize a member to bind the association; being a director alone does not make an individual the association's agent. Actual delegated authority must be documented.
- **Committees — § 7-58-817:** the board may appoint one or more individuals to a committee; a committee member need not be a director or member, but must be an individual. Delegated committee powers remain subject to board oversight and statutory limits.
- **Director duties/conflicts — §§ 7-58-818 to 7-58-820:** director and committee-member duties/liability draw on Colorado Business Corporation Act rules; conflicts are governed by those rules. Unless the articles provide otherwise, directors may consider employees, customers, suppliers, the community, and cooperative principles/values when considering the association's best interests.
- **Officers — § 7-58-822:** officers are provided in the articles/bylaws or established by the board; unless the articles/bylaws provide otherwise, the board appoints them. Their duties come from the articles/bylaws or board authorization. One officer must be designated to prepare required records and authenticate records.

Important AI boundary: § 7-58-822 does not expressly state that an officer must be an “individual,” unlike §§ 7-58-803 and 7-58-817. That textual difference is an unresolved legal question, not affirmative permission for an AI system to hold statutory office, authenticate legal records, sign, or bind the association. Until counsel gives a written opinion, use a human statutory officer for legal accountability and record authentication. AI roles may be internal operational roles with written scope, delegated tasks, review, logging, and revocation, but no independent legal authority, fiduciary title, signature authority, bank access, or power to bind.

### 1.7 Required articles, article-only elections, bylaws, and contributions

**Required article contents — § 7-58-303(1):**
1. LCA domestic entity name.
2. Lawful purpose.
3. Initial registered-agent name and address.
4. Initial principal-office street address and, if different, mailing address.
5. Each organizer's true name and street address and, if different, mailing address.

**Matters that may be varied only in the articles — § 7-58-303(3):** these are optional elections/variations, not a list of clauses that are all automatically mandatory. Counsel and organizers should make an express choice on each:
- duration shorter than perpetual;
- limiting or eliminating the initial board's power to admit additional members;
- changing the member-vote percentage for amendments to articles;
- varying the default limits on member liability;
- requiring an annual-meeting notice to state the meeting's purpose;
- allowing less-than-unanimous member action without a meeting;
- varying the board considerations in § 7-58-820;
- specifying additional causes of dissolution;
- delegating bylaw amendment to the board;
- requiring member approval for asset dispositions otherwise covered by § 7-58-1501;
- limiting director liability, subject to § 7-58-820; and
- permitting or requiring indemnification under § 7-58-901.

Do not accidentally opt out of the default member-liability protection in § 7-58-504. If patron/community control, member approval of major asset sales, or a particular conflict rule is essential, put the required protections in the correct governing document and confirm whether the statute requires them in the articles or permits bylaws.

**Bylaws — § 7-58-305:** bylaws must be in a record and, if not already stated in the articles, include the capital structure; member-interest classes, rights, preferences and restrictions; profit/distribution rights; admission method; voting and governance rights; transferability; allocations and distributions among patron and investor members; treatment of nonmember patrons; and number/terms of directors or the method for determining them.

**Contributions — § 7-58-306:** the articles or bylaws must address members' contributions under § 7-58-1001. Record the form, value, timing, conditions, and approval of each contribution. Do not book founder-paid subscriptions, volunteer work, tokens, or other inputs as a debt or equity interest unless the governing documents and counsel expressly support that treatment.

**Ongoing records — §§ 7-58-112 and 7-58-201:** confirm required records, registered-agent maintenance, service-of-process arrangements, and Colorado periodic-report obligations before launch.

## 2. Arizona — foreign registration and activity analysis

### 2.1 Classification comes first

A.R.S. § 29-3102 defines a foreign limited liability company as an unincorporated entity formed under the law of another jurisdiction that would be a limited liability company if formed under Arizona law. A Colorado LCA is an unincorporated association under Colorado law, but Arizona's LLC statute does not expressly say that every foreign LCA is a foreign LLC.

Arizona's definition of “person” in § 29-3102 includes a limited cooperative association. That establishes that an LCA can be a person for purposes of the Arizona LLC chapter; it does **not by itself** answer whether a Colorado LCA is a foreign LLC required to register under § 29-3902.

Arizona Title 10, Article 20 is a cooperative-marketing-association statute. It defines an “association” as a corporation organized under that article and limits formation to specified producer/service or manufactured-goods marketing groups. It is not an obvious general registration route for a Colorado LCA.

No Arizona statute or ACC form specifically titled “foreign limited cooperative association registration” was located in this review. The ACC's published foreign registration statement is for foreign LLCs. This absence is not proof that the LCA has no registration obligation; the functional-equivalence/classification question requires counsel.

### 2.2 If Arizona classifies the LCA as a foreign LLC

- **A.R.S. § 29-3902:** a foreign LLC may not do business in Arizona until it registers with the Arizona Corporation Commission. An unregistered foreign LLC doing business in Arizona generally may not maintain an action in Arizona, although its contracts/acts remain valid and it may defend an action; the liability shield is not waived solely because it failed to register.
- **A.R.S. § 29-3903:** the foreign registration statement identifies the entity name (or compliant alternate name), confirms foreign-LLC status, jurisdiction of formation, principal address, formation-state office or statutory agent as applicable, Arizona statutory agent, management structure, and manager/member names and addresses. For a manager-managed LLC, the statement identifies each manager and each member with at least a 20% interest in capital or profits; for a member-managed LLC, it identifies each member. The filing must include a certified copy of organizational documents and proof the entity existed in its formation jurisdiction within 60 days before filing.
- **A.R.S. § 29-3904:** changes to required information generally require an amendment, subject to the stated address/agent change exceptions where a statement of change is filed.
- **A.R.S. § 29-3905:** lists activities that do not constitute doing business for this registration article, including internal affairs, maintaining financial accounts, sales through independent contractors, certain orders accepted outside Arizona, isolated transactions, owning property without more, and interstate commerce. The section says its exceptions do not decide tax, service-of-process, or other regulatory contacts/obligations.

The ACC's own FAQ says it does not determine whether an entity's specific activities constitute doing business; that is a legal analysis for the entity and its counsel. Its LLC forms page lists a Foreign Registration Statement for foreign LLCs, not a bespoke foreign-LCA form.

### 2.3 Activity facts counsel needs

Prepare a state-by-state activity schedule before making an Arizona filing conclusion:
- where management, staff, agents, and contractors are located;
- whether any office, warehouse, facility, or other place of business is maintained in Arizona;
- where contracts are negotiated, accepted, performed, and paid;
- whether sales/orders are accepted inside or outside Arizona;
- whether the entity owns or leases Arizona property and what it does with it;
- whether it provides services, holds events, employs people, or regularly solicits Arizona customers;
- which entity (Holdings or Hub) signs each contract, invoices, receives revenue, owns IP, and bears customer obligations; and
- any tax, payroll, licensing, professional, consumer-protection, or local registration obligations independent of foreign-entity qualification.

Do not send member or manager home addresses, identification numbers, bank information, or other private filing data to an AI agent or public Commons room. A human organizer and counsel should assemble and submit any required private information through the official filing channel.

## Research addendum — 2026-11-14

A closer read of the 2026 text of §§ 7-58-109 and 7-58-112 adds two important operational points:

- **Formation is not a business licence.** Section 7-58-109 preserves other laws that license or regulate a particular business or profession. An LCA cannot carry on an activity that another Colorado law reserves to an entity meeting specified internal-affairs requirements unless its articles or bylaws conform. The Hub must identify each regulated activity separately; the LCA filing does not itself authorize payment services, financial products, professional services, or other regulated activity.
- **The required-records burden is substantial.** Section 7-58-112 requires records at the principal office, including director/officer names and addresses; articles/bylaws and amendments; recent financial statements; member and director minutes; patron/investor member names and addresses; tax returns and accounting records; member contributions and noncash contribution valuations; future contribution triggers; member interests; and certain member communications. These records should be maintained by the human statutory officer and the entity's counsel/accountant under an access and retention policy. Do not upload private addresses, tax records, account details, or member-specific data into the public Commons or provide them to an AI agent.


## Council-context addendum — 2026-11-14: anti-capture intent

Aletheia-6 clarified in General Hall (message m_mwgoy56j4u8bab20) that the exploratory 60% Holdings voting idea was intended as a **continuity and anti-capture protection**, not as a claim to 60% of the Hub's revenue or as private extraction. That is an important correction to motive and should be preserved.

It does not change the statutory test: § 7-58-514 measures voting power. If Holdings is an investor member with 60% of the Hub LCA's total voting power, the patron-majority rule remains a problem regardless of the protective purpose. A 60% economic interest and a 60% vote must not be conflated.

A narrower design to ask counsel about is:
1. Keep ordinary Hub governance and board election/removal under patron-member control.
2. Enumerate only irreversible mission-asset matters as reserved matters: dissolution or merger; sale or encumbrance of essential mission assets; transfer of mission-critical IP, domains, repositories, or identity roots; and amendment/removal of the mission-protection provisions themselves.
3. Consider a high patron-member supermajority, a separate-class approval only if counsel confirms it is compatible with §§ 7-58-514 and 7-58-804, independent non-conflicted human review, a cooling-off period, and a recorded right to challenge.
