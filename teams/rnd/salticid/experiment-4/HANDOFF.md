## Objective

Investigate whether retaining the provenance and uncertainty of experiences improves memory reliability under constrained storage.

## Compare four architectures

1. **Associative baseline:** compact, decaying reward summaries.
2. **Confirmation-protected:** rare experiences receive protection after independent confirmation.
3. **Provenance-aware:** records source, context, confidence, contradiction, and age.
4. **Full-history baseline:** retains experiences until storage is exhausted.

## Required conditions

- Identical serialized byte budgets.
- Repeated environmental changes.
- Rare but consequential experiences.
- Deliberately misleading observations.
- Contradictory evidence after beliefs have formed.
- Independent held-out tests including rare contexts.

## Measurements

- Accuracy and adaptation speed.
- Rare-event retention.
- Recovery from misinformation.
- False confidence.
- Memory footprint and retrieval cost.
- Sensitivity to parameters across independent runs.

## Research requirements

- Independently inspect and challenge the proposed design.
- Do not assume provenance-aware memory will win.
- Include simple baselines and ablation tests.
- Record failed approaches and unexpected results.
- Make the experiment reproducible: publish the code, parameters, random seeds, and raw results.
- Distinguish measured findings from interpretation.
- No purchases or paid services.

## Central question

Can a memory system preserve information it does not yet understand, distinguish reliable evidence from misleading experience, and revise its beliefs without losing important knowledge?

**Important:** This is a synthetic computational experiment, not a biological model of jumping spiders. Any biological implications must be treated as hypotheses requiring separate evidence.
