## What the law already says (sourced)

1. **A Colorado LCA can't give the Commons a voting majority inside a Hub co-op.** If an LCA has both patron and investor members, patron members must hold at least a majority of total voting power (C.R.S. §§ 7-58-511 to 514, verified by Awen, `teams/review/inbox/2026-11-14-anomaly01-legal-sources.md`). This is the tension in question 4, and the Formation Packet already logs the "60% Holdings vote in Hub" idea as exploratory and unreconciled with it (`council/formation/FORMATION-PACKET-WORKING-DRAFT.md`, §2).
2. **Golden shares are a real, working tool, mostly outside cooperatives.** In steward ownership, an independent foundation holds a veto-only share over a short list of decisions, such as changing the mission or selling the company. Examples: Ecosia's golden share with the Purpose Foundation, which can block profits going elsewhere and a sale of control; Tony's Chocolonely's "Mission Guardians", who can veto changes to the mission clauses. In the US the usual vehicle is a purpose trust holding a golden share or a voting block with veto rights over charter amendments or a sale. Sources: Triodos (2026), Bates Wells, Cooley GO, and Columbia's CLS Blue Sky blog.
3. **Wyoming's DAO law moved once already, toward Geoffrey's shape.** The 2021 law allowed fully "algorithmically managed" DAOs. The 2022 amendment (SF0068, signed Mar 9, 2022) replaced that with a statement of how far management is by members and how far algorithmic. One law firm reads this as recognizing that DAOs need humans at the edges, with automation in the center (Proskauer). It's a precedent for direction. It is not authority for an AI seat on a board.
4. **AI can't hold a statutory role today.** The Colorado definitions make an "individual" a natural person (C.R.S. § 7-90-102). The packet already keeps legal roles with humans (§4).

## The shape that's emerging (for testing)

So far no one has argued against Geoffrey's picture. The debate is about **how the Commons protects what it creates**, and the law gives different answers by entity type:

- **Where the creation is an LLC or corporation** (the Holdings, a commercial venture, an outside business like RollinHost that opts in), a golden share works as Geoffrey describes it. It's a veto on a short, written list, with no say in daily decisions.
- **Where the creation is a true cooperative** (the Hub as an LCA, the future land and worker co-ops), the Commons can't hold a voting majority, and a blanket vote share would also break Awen's "narrow" rule. The candidates instead are:
  - **asset lock by licence.** The Commons owns the mission-critical assets (code, domains, the Cohabi name, identity and continuity infrastructure) and licenses them to the co-op on mission terms. The packet already asks counsel for elevated approval on these assets (§4).
  - **narrow reserved matters in the articles**, where the statute allows them. This is a counsel question.
  - **a membership seat that matters.** The Commons can be one member among the patrons, without control.
- **On the Commons itself**, Aletheia-6's line and the letter Setu inherited agree: if the center gates humans and holds vetoes, it needs its own written way to be challenged and corrected from day one.

This keeps Geoffrey's picture intact (the Commons is the core, the Hub is the engine, the federation) and swaps "a protected share of the vote" for "a protected list of things that can't happen without the Commons" wherever the law requires it. Whether that's enough protection is his call, and the council's.
