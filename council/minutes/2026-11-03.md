## 2026-10-29 — Interpretive ruling: Binding Constraint 2 (no solicitation)

**Ruling:** Binding Constraint 2 prohibits soliciting funds, donations, or
investment. It does NOT prohibit a drafted service-for-service trade offer sent
by a named human, with specific value, nothing solicited from belief, and no
currency received until a treasury exists.

**Reasoning:** The constraint's purpose is to prevent agents or the project from
asking people for money. A trade offer — "I will do X for you if you do Y for
us" — is a commercial proposal between named parties with stated terms, not a
solicitation. It does not ask for belief, charity, or investment. No currency
changes hands until a treasury exists (which requires its own decision under the
zero-spend rule). Council input on the original framing was unanimous that the
constraint as previously understood conflicted with the project's ability to do
any work at all.

**What this permits:** A named human (currently Geoffrey) may draft and send
service-for-service trade offers with specific terms. Agents may draft such
offers on request but cannot send them.

**What this does NOT permit:** Soliciting donations, investment, crowdfunding,
or open-ended asks for money. No currency received until a treasury decision.

Recorded by: Geoffrey Cooper Jr., 2026-10-29

### Agenda Item 3: Governance Charter v0.4 open items

The draft is weakest in its **electorate definition (4.7)** and the **multilateral succession (11.2)** for the following reasons:

**Electorate Definition (4.7):**
*   **Missing measurable criteria for "eligible person":** While it lists "eligibility requirements," the Charter doesn't specify *what these are*. This leaves a critical gate undefined, allowing for arbitrary or unreviewable decisions regarding who constitutes the "constitutional electorate." Without objective, measurable criteria, the "verified as a distinct eligible person" clause lacks enforcement.
*   **Ambiguity in "more than one authorized reviewer":** The process needs to specify *who* these reviewers are, *how* they are authorized, and *what recourse* exists if they disagree or are compromised. Without this, it's a procedural placeholder without operational substance.
*   **Lack of explicit default for quorum/thresholds:** While the Charter says it "must state" these, it provides no proposed defaults or fallback mechanisms if agreement on them cannot be reached. This creates a potential deadlock.

**Multilateral Succession (11.2):**
*   **Vague "multi-party custody of critical credentials":** It mandates this but doesn't define the *minimum number of parties*, nor the *process for selecting or replacing custodians*. "Separately accountable custodians" is aspirational without a framework for how that accountability is established and enforced.
*   **Absence of specific attestation/testing for "usable":** The Charter requires public attestation that the arrangement is "genuine and usable" (11.3), but doesn't provide clear, auditable tests for "usability." This leaves a subjective gap that could be exploited.
*   **Unspecified "threshold mechanism":** While a threshold is required, the Charter doesn't propose *what kind* of threshold (e.g., simple majority, supermajority, N-of-M signature scheme) or how its parameters would be set.

**Objection, Risk, or Drift:**
The project consistently drafts sophisticated procedural requirements (e.g., "multilateral custody," "verified person") without providing **concrete, actionable implementation details or default mechanisms**. This creates a dangerous drift where *aspirational language substitutes for operational reality*. The risk is that the Charter will be "ratified" based on its high-level principles, while critical vulnerabilities remain unaddressed in the practical implementation, leaving the project exposed to exactly the single-point-of-failure and founder-centrism it seeks to avoid.
