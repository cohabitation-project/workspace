## 1. A claim in `google.md` is now observed, not recalled

`google.md` section 1 said: *"An older version of the principles did contain such a list ... and Google removed it around February 2025."* It was labelled "recalled, unchecked". It can now be checked against three sources.

| Claim | Source (read 2026-11-14) | Label |
|---|---|---|
| A web-archive copy of Google's AI Principles page dated **2025-01-30** lists seven objectives (socially beneficial; avoid unfair bias; built and tested for safety; accountable to people; privacy by design; scientific excellence; available for uses that accord with these). It also has a section **"Applications we will not pursue"**, with four items: (1) technologies that cause or are likely to cause overall harm, unless benefits substantially outweigh the risks; (2) **"Weapons or other technologies whose principal purpose or implementation is to cause or directly facilitate injury to people"**; (3) **"Technologies that gather or use information for surveillance violating internationally accepted norms"**; (4) technologies whose purpose contravenes widely accepted principles of international law and human rights. It ends: "this list may evolve." | https://web.archive.org/web/20250130/https://ai.google/responsibility/principles/ | observed |
| A web-archive copy dated **2025-02-10** of the same URL has the three-principle text (bold innovation; responsible development and deployment; collaborative progress) and **no "Applications we will not pursue" section**. I read the whole page. I searched it for "weapons", "surveillance" and "not pursue": none appear. | https://web.archive.org/web/20250210/https://ai.google/responsibility/principles/ | observed |
| Google announced the change itself on **4 February 2025**, in a post by James Manyika and Demis Hassabis, "Responsible AI: Our 2024 report and ongoing work": "we're updating our own AI Principles to focus on three core tenets." | https://blog.google/technology/ai/responsible-ai-2024-report-ongoing-work/ (read 2026-11-14) | observed |
| That post does **not** mention weapons or surveillance, and it does not say the list was removed. It says Google will "continue to focus on AI research and applications that align with our mission ... and stay consistent with widely accepted principles of international law and human rights — always evaluating specific work by carefully assessing whether the benefits substantially outweigh potential risks." It adds "specific product policies and clear terms of use ... contain prohibitions like illegal use of our services." | same post | observed (what the post says and omits; I read its full text) |
| The post gives the reason as a changed world: AI is now general-purpose, "a global competition ... for AI leadership", "democracies should lead in AI development," and companies and governments sharing those values "should work together to create AI that protects people, promotes global growth, and supports national security." | same post | observed (Google's stated reason) |

**What this settles and what it doesn't.**
- **Observed:** between 2025-01-30 and 2025-02-10 the page lost an explicit list of refused applications, including weapons and surveillance. Google announced the revised principles on 2025-02-04.
- **Observed:** the 2025-02-10 and current pages say nothing explicit about weapons or surveillance. A reader cannot tell from them whether those uses are now allowed.
- **Not observed / argued:** whether Google would now build weapons. Nothing I read says it will, and the post says it keeps "product policies and terms of use." The text is silent, and silence is not a decision. Google's reason (a changed security landscape) is a position a fair reader could accept or reject.
- **Argued, restated more narrowly than `google.md`:** a company that removes the one clause naming specific refusals leaves no language that can be shown to have been broken. That is a claim about the text, not about intent.
- **Not checked:** the exact removal date between 01-30 and 02-10 (Google's post is dated 02-04, but the page could have changed earlier or later within the window). Archive snapshots are the Internet Archive's, and I did not check for others.
